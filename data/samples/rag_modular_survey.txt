# Title: Modular Retrieval-Augmented Generation: Paradigms, Routing, and Limitations

## Section 1: Introduction and Naive RAG Shortcomings
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) addresses the fundamental knowledge-cutoff and hallucination problems inherent to Large Language Models (LLMs). Early systems followed the "Naive RAG" paradigm: a static retrieve-then-read approach. Documents are divided into fixed chunks, embedded into a vector database, and retrieved using dense cosine similarity against the user query.

However, empirical evaluations demonstrate significant failure modes in Naive RAG:
1. Low Precision and Irrelevant Chunks: Unfiltered retrieval injects noisy context into LLM context windows, reducing faithfulness by 34%.
2. Outdated or Conflicting Knowledge: When conflicting facts exist, LLMs often default to parametric memory biases.
3. Multi-Hop Reasoning Blind Spots: Single-round cosine similarity cannot connect distributed evidence across multiple sections or papers.

## Section 2: Modular and Advanced RAG Paradigms
To solve these limitations, the Modular RAG architecture decouples retrieval into specialized sub-modules:
- Pre-Retrieval Module: Query reformulation, hypothetical document embeddings (HyDE), and sub-query routing.
- Retrieval Module: Hybrid dense-sparse indexing (e.g., BM25 combined with all-MiniLM-L6-v2) and multi-vector search.
- Post-Retrieval Module: Reranking, relevance filtering, and context compression to filter out irrelevant chunks before prompting the generator.

Empirical benchmarks demonstrate that Modular RAG reduces hallucination rates from 23.4% down to 6.2% on open-domain QA tasks, while boosting factual accuracy by 28.7%.

## Section 3: Identified Research Gaps & Open Challenges
Despite recent advancements, three critical research gaps persist in the literature:
1. Multimodal and Tabular Reasoning Gap: Most RAG pipelines discard or severely mutilate tabular datasets and figures, leading to high error rates on quantitative synthesis questions.
2. Latency and Cost Overhead: Multi-stage routing and reranking increase inference latency by 3.8x compared to standard LLM generation, limiting real-time deployment in high-throughput enterprise systems.
3. Dynamic Evidence Verification: Static vector indexes cannot dynamically verify whether retrieved claims from the web have been superseded or contradicted by newer empirical benchmarks.
